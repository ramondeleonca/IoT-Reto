## Description of the changes

### 1. Why the estimator is a module and not one line

Velocity is `distance / time`, and that single expression is where most monocular speed
projects fail. `speed.py` handles five specific failure modes explicitly, each of which
produced a real bug or a real inaccuracy during development:

* **Timestamps are supplied, never assumed.** `frame_index / fps` is wrong for any
  variable-frame-rate source, which includes phone recordings and, critically, the Raspberry Pi
  Camera Module, whose `CAP_PROP_FPS` is unreliable and which carries no hardware timestamp.
  The estimator therefore differences real times. A test drives a constant-velocity track at
  jittery 20–40 FPS intervals and asserts the true speed is still recovered.
* **Smoothing is applied to positions, not to speeds.** Differencing a noisy position series
  amplifies noise; a local quadratic fit suppresses it while preserving acceleration. A test
  asserts that a constant-velocity track passes through the filter untouched in the interior,
  so the filter cannot invent an acceleration of its own.
* **Consecutive steps are gated before anything else.** See the defect below.
* **A robust statistic is taken over per-step speeds**, not the endpoint difference. The median
  resists a single outlier; an endpoint difference is exactly the difference of two noisy
  samples and does not.
* **The noise floor is reported alongside every measurement**, derived from the local ground
  resolution and the actual sample interval. This converts "the speeds look noisy at 3 FPS" from
  a mystery into a number, and it is asserted to scale as `1/dt`: a four-times-longer frame
  interval gives four times the floor.

### 2. Defect: smoothing ran before gating

The first implementation smoothed the whole position series and only then discarded implausible
steps. Because a Savitzky-Golay kernel spans five samples, a single teleport — the signature of
a track identity switch — bled into its neighbours and turned one bad sample into three, two of
which then looked plausible and entered the estimate.

Fixed by reordering: gate the raw steps first, then smooth only *within* maximal runs of
plausible steps (`_contiguous_runs`), so a rejected step becomes a hard boundary the kernel
cannot reach across. The test now asserts exactly two rejections for a single teleport, which
pins the ordering: a regression to the old sequence would report three.

### 3. Defect: smoothing applied to irregular sampling

A second failure surfaced on the variable-frame-rate test, which reported 2.033 m/s for a true
2.0 m/s track. The cause is that the Savitzky-Golay coefficients are derived for **evenly
spaced** samples. On intervals varying by a factor of two, the filter is not conservative: the
per-step speeds of a constant-velocity track came out spanning 1.42 to 2.67 instead of all
being equal.

Rather than leave a filter running outside its assumptions, `_sampling_is_regular` measures the
coefficient of variation of the sample intervals and skips smoothing above a configurable
threshold (`max_interval_cv`, default 0.2). The robust statistic then carries the estimate
alone. Skipping a filter whose assumptions do not hold is strictly better than applying it, and
the coefficient of variation was chosen over a min/max ratio because it responds to overall
irregularity rather than to one outlier interval.

### 4. Test defect: noise floor relationship inverted

An assertion claimed that a four-times-shorter frame interval gives a four-times-higher noise
floor. It is the other way round — the floor is proportional to `1/dt`, so the *longer* interval
is worse. The formula in `speed.py` was correct throughout; the test was wrong, and is now
asserted against a window long enough to contain the whole track so that frame rate is the only
variable.

### 5. Documented boundary behaviour instead of a hidden one

Edge replication in the smoother makes the filter exact for a constant and exact for a linear
ramp *in the interior*, but it bends the last two samples because replication implies zero slope
at the boundary — about a third of one step low for a ramp. This is now documented and pinned by
a test. Linear extrapolation would preserve a ramp exactly, but it would also extrapolate a car's
position into free space at the frame edge, which is precisely where tracks begin and end. A
small boundary bias on a car entering or leaving view is the safer failure, and the robust
statistic absorbs it.

### 6. Configuration files

* `config/default.yaml` — detector, tracker, ground-contact point, speed, validity, capture and
  output settings. Kept separate from the camera files so that swapping rigs cannot silently
  change detector or tracker behaviour.
* `config/camera_topdown.yaml` — configuration A, with the `height_m` calibration route as the
  documented starting point and the two-mark route as the fallback.
* `config/camera_roadside.yaml` — configuration B, carrying an explicit statement that a single
  measured distance cannot determine a 2D ground homography and that exactly one of
  `horizon_row`, `height_m` or `pitch_deg` must accompany it.

Notable values and the reasoning recorded in comments: `yolo26n.pt` (STAL targets small objects,
the 1:64 regime); `imgsz: 640` with an explicit note to drop to 320–416 on a Pi Zero 2 W;
`source.mode: stream|burst` for motion-triggered capture; `queue_size: 1` so frames are dropped
rather than queued, keeping latency and timestamps meaningful; and a `max_error_m` of 5 cm that
does real gating work on the roadside rig (it binds at roughly 6 m down the road at 35° and
1.5 m) but very little on the overhead one.

## JUSTIFICATION FOR THE CHANGES

* **A separate `speed.py` with no NumPy-external dependencies.** Speed is a *difference* of
  positions divided by time, so it is the component where estimator quality dominates. Keeping
  it free of OpenCV and torch means it can be tested exhaustively against synthetic ground truth
  — 28 tests with known true speeds — and can run on a 512 MB device. Every accuracy claim in
  the project is ultimately a claim about this file, so it must be verifiable without a camera,
  a model download or a GPU.
* **Gating before smoothing.** The ordering is not stylistic. A tracker identity switch is the
  most common corruption of a real track, and the original ordering amplified one bad sample
  into three. Confining contamination to the step that contains the outlier is what makes the
  robust statistic sufficient on its own.
* **Refusing to smooth irregular sampling.** The alternative — applying a uniform-sample filter
  to jittery Raspberry Pi timestamps — introduces a systematic error into the exact quantity
  being measured. Measuring the irregularity and backing off is the honest option, and it is
  consistent with the geometry module's treatment of the error budget.
* **Reporting a noise floor rather than a bare number.** A speed without its uncertainty invites
  over-interpretation, and on constrained hardware the resolution limit is often the dominant
  term. Reporting it makes the constraint legible and prevents blaming the detector for a
  limitation imposed by frame rate and ground scale.
* **Refusal as a valid outcome.** `SpeedEstimate` carries a `reason` and returns no value when
  the data cannot support one. Emitting a confident number computed from two samples a
  millisecond apart is the failure this guards against.
* **Configuration separated by lifetime.** Detector and tracker settings describe the software
  and are shared; camera geometry describes one physical rig and is per-file. Splitting them
  along that line means `--config config/camera_roadside.yaml` changes only what it should.
* **The roadside config documents what it cannot do.** Since the two-point workflow the user
  chose is information-limited, the file states the limitation and the three ways to satisfy it
  rather than presenting a single knob that would sometimes silently be wrong.
