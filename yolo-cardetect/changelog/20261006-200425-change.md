## Description of the changes

### 1. Both mounting configurations, behind one interface

`GroundPlaneProjector` is the abstraction the rest of the pipeline will use. It answers three
questions and nothing else needs to know which rig is in use:

1. `to_ground(pts)` — where is this image point on the road, in metres?
2. `scale(pts)` / `position_sigma_m(pts)` — how accurate is that answer *here*?
3. `is_valid(pts)` — is this point usable at all?

Question 2 is the one naive implementations skip, and it is what makes a Raspberry Pi
deployment honest: the speed estimator can down-weight or reject samples instead of averaging
garbage into a number.

* **`TopDownProjector`** (configuration A, overhead pole) wraps an `AffineGroundPlane`. Two
  constructors matter: `from_geometry(intrinsics, height_m)`, which needs no calibration marks
  at all because the scale is simply `h / f`, and `from_measured_distance(...)`, the
  tape-measure workflow. An affine rather than projective model is used deliberately: when the
  optical axis is within a few degrees of vertical the projective terms are negligible, while
  their *estimate* from a handful of clicked points is very unstable.
* **`RoadsideProjector`** (configuration B, roadside pole) is backed by a full `CameraModel`
  and offers `from_geometry`, `from_measured_distance` and `from_homography` (the ArUco /
  four-point path).

`build_projector(cfg)` selects between them from one config line, and both round-trip through
`to_config()` / `from_config()`.

### 2. Geometry with a closed-form ground homography

For the road frame (origin at the camera nadir, `+X` across, `+Y` down, `+Z` along the road)
the ground-to-image map is exactly `K · [r1  r3  −R·C]`, where `r1` and `r3` are the first and
third columns of the rotation. This is implemented directly rather than by composing
`cv2.projectPoints` calls, and is verified against OpenCV to 1e-12 px.

### 3. Local resolution and an explicit error budget

`ground_scale()` now returns the **analytic** projective Jacobian's singular values:
`m_per_px_min/max`, `m_per_px_long/lat`, `anisotropy`, `px_per_m`. The analytic form replaced a
finite-difference version whose ~1e-3 relative truncation error propagated into the calibration
solvers. From this, `min_speed_sigma()` gives a hard lower bound on speed noise for a given
frame interval and detector jitter — the number that explains why 3 FPS on a Pi Zero produces
noisy speeds without that being a detector failure.

For a roadside rig, `usable_range_m()` reports how far down the road the rig can actually
measure for a configured error budget, and `is_valid()` gates detections accordingly. The gate
that does real work is `Z > 0`: a ground homography is defined for *every* pixel, so a point
above the horizon yields a finite but **negative** road distance — a position behind the
camera. It looks harmless until it is differenced into a speed.

### 4. Calibration solvers, and the tape-measure workflow

`solve_pitch_from_horizon`, `solve_height_from_ground_scale` and `solve_pitch_from_ground_scale`
invert the forward projection. Both scale solvers route through the *same* `CameraModel` code
path used at run time, so a calibration is exact with respect to actual measurements rather
than with respect to a re-derived formula. They are mutually exact inverses to 1e-10.

For the user's chosen "tape measure and two marks" workflow, the honest limitation is handled
explicitly: **a single measured distance cannot determine a 2D ground homography** — it yields
only a similarity (rotation + scale). `RoadsideProjector.from_measured_distance` therefore
requires the measured distance *plus* one of a horizon row, a measured lens height, or a known
tilt, and raises a descriptive error if given none, rather than silently guessing. A wrong
height biases every speed by exactly the height ratio, so guessing is unacceptable here.

### 5. Signs: the part that consumed most of the work

The rotation matrix was wrong four times in a row, in ways that survived every naive check. The
failure modes are recorded in the module docstring and pinned by tests, because each of them is
individually plausible:

* The **full transpose** of the matrix. `cv2.projectPoints` agreed with it to 1e-12 px, because
  OpenCV was simply handed the same wrong matrix. This was only exposed by a physical
  invariant: the point where the optical axis meets the road must project to the image centre.
* Negating the **wrong row** to try to fix the transpose, twice, at different times.
* A **horizon formula read off the wrong quantity**. The vanishing point of the road direction
  sits *below* the principal point and is therefore inside the frame for any real roadside
  camera. The horizon is a different line, and getting this wrong placed the horizon in the
  middle of the pavement.
* A **mirrored overhead affine plane**, which flipped every overhead car's Z coordinate so
  tracks ran backwards while `m_per_px` — derived from a determinant — still looked perfectly
  healthy.

Three physical oracles were adopted to settle all of these, and they are asserted in the test
suite:

1. the optical-axis/road intersection projects to the image centre;
2. nearer ground appears **lower** in the frame (monotonically larger `v`);
3. a `+X` offset appears to the **right**.

Exactly one proper rotation satisfies all three, found by exhaustive enumeration. It has
`R[1,2] = -sin p`, `R[2,2] = +cos p`, and the horizon identity `v = cy - fy·tan(p)` follows.

### 6. Documented degeneracies rather than hidden ones

* **Gimbal lock at vertical.** The pitch/yaw/roll parameterisation is singular when looking
  exactly straight down; the generic composition produced a rank-deficient homography (a zero
  column) that surfaced as a confusing "degenerate camera pose" error. The overhead pose is now
  a documented special case built from its axis alignment, reachable via `Extrinsics.overhead()`.
* **The overhead pose is a reflection** (`det = -1`), deliberately. An overhead camera must be
  mounted at one particular end of the track, which fixes the handedness of a frame in which
  image "down" means increasing road distance. The alternative would make every overhead car
  appear to drive backwards. A reflection preserves lengths, so speeds are unaffected.
* **The two rigs' Z conventions are opposite at the vertical limit.** The overhead model's `+Z`
  is the direction of increasing image `v`; the roadside model's `+Z` is away from the camera.
  The near-vertical limit matches `U_Z = -Z·cos(ε)` with error `O(ε)`, which is asserted. This
  is a coordinate convention, not a defect, and velocity magnitudes are unaffected.

### 7. Edge deployment constraints drove the dependency layering

`geometry` (and later `speed`) depend on **NumPy only**; OpenCV is used opportunistically when
importable, with a NumPy fallback for the distortion model, the DLT homography and the pose
refinement (a hand-written Levenberg–Marquardt). `import cardetect.geometry` therefore never
pulls in torch, which is what makes a 512 MB Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W target viable and the suite
runnable headless.

`lap` is pinned explicitly in `pyproject.toml`. It is *not* an ultralytics dependency, but
ultralytics imports it lazily inside the tracker modules, so its absence only surfaces when
tracking starts — a confusing runtime failure that was reproduced during environment validation.

## JUSTIFICATION FOR THE CHANGES

* **Affine model for the overhead rig, projective for the roadside rig.** This is a
  conditioning decision, not a simplification. Under a near-vertical camera the projective
  terms are negligible but their estimate from few noisy points is very unstable, so the
  6-parameter affine model is the better estimator of the geometry that actually exists. Under
  a shallow pitch the opposite holds and a single metres-per-pixel figure would be wrong by a
  factor of several across the frame.
* **Exposing per-point resolution instead of one global scale.** Speed is a *difference* of
  positions divided by time, so accuracy is set by the local resolution at each sample. On a
  roadside rig that varies by an order of magnitude across the frame; reporting it is what
  allows confidence-weighted estimates and honest error bars, and it converts "the speeds look
  noisy at 3 FPS" from a mystery into a number.
* **Physical invariants as the test strategy.** Four incorrect sign conventions all passed
  orthonormality and `cv2.projectPoints` agreement. Invariants that encode how a real camera
  must behave are the only checks that catch that class of error, which is why the suite is
  built around them rather than around golden values.
* **Closed-form homography rather than repeated `projectPoints` calls.** The homography is
  needed anyway for vectorised projection and for the vanishing-point diagnostics; deriving it
  in closed form and verifying it against OpenCV keeps one source of truth and avoids an FFI
  round trip per detection, which matters on a Pi.
* **Analytic Jacobian rather than finite differences.** The truncation error was ~1e-3
  relative; harmless for display but not for the calibration solvers that consume it, where it
  becomes a systematic speed bias.
* **Refusing to guess in the two-point calibration.** The alternative — inventing a plausible
  pitch or height — produces speeds that are wrong by a constant factor, which looks entirely
  reasonable and would survive to a final report.
* **NumPy-only core.** The deployment target has 512 MB of RAM. Keeping torch out of the core
  import graph is the difference between the geometry being usable on the device and not.
